Pharmacovigilance

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activity of detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing adverse effects and other medicine-related problems. This page follows how a safety concern travels from one patient to a regulatory decision.

Signal detection: PRR, ROR and chi-square

Spontaneous-report databases can be screened for drug–event pairs reported more often than expected. These measures compare reporting patterns. They are screening tools.

PRR = [a / (a + b)] / [c / (c + d)] ROR = (a / b) / (c / d) = (a·d) / (b·c) χ² = N·(a·d − b·c)² / [(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)] N = a + b + c + d

PRR compares the proportion of a drug’s reports that name the event with that proportion for all other drugs. ROR compares the odds. χ² tests whether the difference is larger than chance alone would give, under independence assumptions.

Reading the result carefully

  • A commonly cited screening rule (from the literature, not a law) flags a pair when PRR ≥ 2, χ² ≥ 4 and at least 3 cases. Thresholds differ between organisations.
  • A flagged pair is a hypothesis to investigate, not a finding that the drug caused the event.
  • Reporting is voluntary and incomplete; it is affected by publicity, drug age, disease severity and reporting habits. Reporting rates are not incidence rates.
  • Confounding, such as the underlying illness the drug is used for, can create a signal with no causal link.
  • A signal with a low PRR is not proof of safety, either.

ESTABLISHED CONCEPTS

The pharmacovigilance chain, Naranjo scoring and disproportionality statistics are standard, widely taught methods. Specific thresholds, reporting requirements and terminology vary by regulator and change over time; check the current guidance of the relevant authority. This page is educational and does not replace regulatory guidance or clinical judgement. The case example is fictional.
